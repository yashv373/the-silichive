CMOS Fundamentals

category: fundamentals status: active

Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor (CMOS) technology forms the backbone of modern digital integrated circuits. Understanding CMOS fundamentals is essential for any work in VLSI design, from logic synthesis to physical implementation.

The CMOS Inverter

The CMOS inverter is the most basic building block. It consists of a PMOS pull-up network and an NMOS pull-down network connected in a complementary fashion. 

         VDD
          │
       ┌──┴──┐
       │ PMOS│
       └──┬──┘
          ├──── Vout
       ┌──┴──┐
       │ NMOS│
       └──┬──┘
          │
         GND

  Vin ──┤ gate (both transistors)

Key characteristics:

  • Full rail-to-rail swing — output reaches VDD and GND cleanly
  • Zero static power — in steady state, one transistor is always off (ideally)
  • High noise margins — inherent to complementary topology
  • Ratioless logic — sizing affects speed, not correctness

Voltage Transfer Characteristic

The VTC of a CMOS inverter maps input voltage to output voltage. The key parameters are:

Parameter Description Typical Value
VIL Maximum input low voltage ~0.4 × VDD
VIH Minimum input high voltage ~0.6 × VDD
VOL Output low voltage ~0 V (GND)
VOH Output high voltage ~VDD
VM Switching threshold ~VDD/2

Noise Margins

Noise margins quantify how much noise a circuit can tolerate while still functioning correctly:

  • NMH = VOH − VIH — high noise margin
  • NML = VIL − VOL — low noise margin

A symmetric inverter (where PMOS is sized ~2–3× NMOS width to compensate for mobility difference) yields equal noise margins. This is generally the starting point for standard cell design.

Power Dissipation

CMOS power has three components:

Dynamic Power

The dominant component in traditional CMOS: 

P_dynamic = α × C_L × VDD² × f

Where α is the activity factor, CL is load capacitance, and f is clock frequency. This is why voltage scaling is so effective — power scales quadratically with VDD.

Short-Circuit Power

During switching, both PMOS and NMOS are momentarily on, creating a direct path from VDD to GND. Typically 5–10% of dynamic power. Minimized by matching rise/fall times.

Leakage Power

Increasingly dominant at advanced nodes. Sources include subthreshold leakage, gate leakage, and junction leakage. This is the fundamental challenge driving FinFET adoption and advanced device architectures.

Key Insight

As technology nodes shrink, the balance between dynamic and leakage power shifts dramatically. At 7nm and below, leakage management often dominates design decisions more than performance optimization.

Scaling Considerations

Dennard scaling — which promised that power density remains constant as transistors shrink — broke down around the 90nm node. The consequences:

  • VDD scaling slowed (subthreshold leakage prevents aggressive reduction)
  • Power density increased with each node
  • Multi-core architectures became necessary
  • Dark silicon emerged as a design constraint
  • Novel device structures (FinFET, GAA) became essential

The end of Dennard scaling didn't end Moore's Law — it changed what we optimize for. Performance-per-watt replaced raw performance as the primary design metric.
